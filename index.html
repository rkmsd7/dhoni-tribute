The story

Before he was Captain Cool

He was a goalkeeper. The games teacher at DAV Jawahar Vidya Mandir in Shyamali, Ranchi, needed a wicketkeeper for the school side and thought the boy who threw himself around in goal might as well throw himself around behind the stumps. Mahendra Singh Dhoni, the youngest child of a MECON junior manager who had moved from the Kumaon hills for the job, did not grow up in a cricket academy, did not play age-group cricket for India, and did not come to anyone's attention until he was well into his twenties.

What he did instead was hit the ball very hard, in a way that nobody had taught him and most coaches would have corrected. His first-class debut for Bihar came in January 2000; his first job came a year later, as a Travelling Ticket Examiner at Kharagpur station in West Bengal, playing for the Railways on the side. He kept that job for three seasons. The selectors did not call.

They called after a BCCI talent scout watched him in the 2003–04 Deodhar and Duleep Trophies and after two hundreds against Pakistan A in Nairobi in 2004. His debut, in December 2004, ended in a run-out for nought. Four months later, promoted to No.3 against Pakistan at Visakhapatnam, he made 148 and the long hair and the cross-bat heaves were suddenly the most imitated thing in Indian cricket. By October 2005 he had 183 not out and by April 2006 he was the No.1 one-day batter in the world.

The rest is the longest chapter in Indian cricket: the captaincy at 26, the 2007 World T20, the World Cup, the Champions Trophy, five IPL titles, a retirement announced at 19:29 on Independence Day and a Hall of Fame cap. This site holds all of it, match by match, with sources. Scroll on.
